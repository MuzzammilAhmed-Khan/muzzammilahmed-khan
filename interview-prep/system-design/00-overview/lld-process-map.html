Module 00 · Overview

The LLD pipeline

Taught · not drilled Session 1 · 2026-10-05 Worked example: Design a Deck of Cards

Every low-level design problem you will ever be handed is the same nine steps in the same order. The steps are not the hard part — each one is small. The hard part is that nobody tells you they exist, so you improvise, and improvising under interview pressure produces a pile of classes nobody can defend.

This page is the map. It exists so that every later topic has an address: when you learn composition, you will know it lives in step 5.

How to use this page

Read it once, end to end, without trying to memorise anything. You are not being drilled today. The goal is only that you can answer “what are we even doing?” before we start doing it one brick at a time.

Never played cards?

Then this example will fight you, and the fight is not the lesson. The same nine steps are run on something you have definitely used — the Up Next queue in a music app — over on the same pipeline, in music. Nothing here is required reading first.

If you do read both, read that one second: it ends with the two designs side by side, and the one relationship where they disagree is worth more than either example alone.

01 The nine steps

Learn the shape first. The parenthetical is the question the step answers.

  1. Clarify the requirements — what am I actually building? Ask until the problem is bounded. Write down what is in scope and, more importantly, what is out.
  2. Extract the nouns — what things are being talked about? Harvest every noun from the statement and from your own clarifying answers. Do not judge them yet.
  3. Filter the nouns — which of these are really classes? Each noun is a class, an attribute, an enum, or noise. Most are not classes. This is where beginners create fifteen classes for a problem that needs five.
  4. Assign one responsibility each — what is this class for? One sentence per class, in the first person. If you need the word “and” twice, you have two classes.
  5. Find the relationships — who holds whom, and how tightly? Owns, references, or merely uses. This step is where most of the real design decisions hide.
  6. Draw it — does the whole thing hang together? A diagram makes a bad design look bad. That is its entire job.
  7. Code it — do the signatures actually work? Write the classes. Half of all design mistakes only become visible when you try to type the method signature.
  8. Run it — does it behave? In an interview this is a dry run on paper. On your own machine it is javac.
  9. Critique it — where will this break? Name the weaknesses yourself. In an interview, doing this unprompted is the single strongest signal you can send.
The one rule

Steps 1 to 5 happen before you write a single line of code. The most common way to fail an LLD interview is to start at step 7.

02 Step 1 — Clarify the requirements

The prompt was three words: “Design a deck of cards.” That is not a specification, it is an invitation to ask questions. Here are the ones worth asking, and the answers I am going to assume:

QuestionAnswer I will design to
Standard 52-card deck, or jokers / multiple packs / custom decks?Standard 52. No jokers.
Does dealing remove cards, or just look at them?Removes. A dealt card leaves the deck.
Do we model players and hands?No. The deck hands out cards; who receives them is somebody else’s problem.
Does it need to serve a specific game’s rules?No game. No scoring, no trump suits.
Is a discard pile reshuffled back in?No. Out of scope.
Multiple threads dealing at once?No. Single-threaded.
How is shuffling specified?Must be swappable — a fair shuffle now, something else later.

In scope: build a 52-card deck, shuffle it, deal one or many cards, report how many are left, print a card readably, compare two cards for equality.

Out of scope: players, hands, games, scoring, jokers, multiple decks, discard piles, persistence, concurrency.

Why this step matters

Notice that the last question — how shuffling is specified — is the only one whose answer changes the structure of the design. “Must be swappable” is what forces an interface into existence in step 5. Requirements are where structure comes from; this is why you cannot skip to coding.

03 Step 2 — Extract the nouns

Everything the problem and my answers mention, harvested without judgement:

deck · card · suit · rank · colour · value · joker · player · hand · game · shuffle · deal · top of the deck · discard pile

Fourteen nouns. The design has five types. That ratio is normal and it is the point of the next step.

04 Step 3 — Filter the nouns

Every noun gets exactly one verdict. This table is the most valuable thing on this page.

NounVerdictWhy
cardclassHas attributes, is compared, is printed. A thing in its own right.
deckclassOwns a collection and has behaviour over it.
suitenumA closed set of exactly four, and each one carries data (symbol, colour).
rankenumA closed set of exactly thirteen, each carrying a label and a value.
colourenum, nested in SuitNot independent — it is a property of a suit. Hearts are red; there is no free-floating colour.
valueattribute of RankA number on a rank, not a thing that exists by itself.
shufflemethod — and an interfaceA verb, so normally just a method. It earns a type only because step 1 said the algorithm must be swappable.
dealmethod on DeckA verb with exactly one implementation. No type needed.
top of the decknoise — it is a conventionBecomes a one-line decision: the top is the end of the list, so dealing is O(1).
player, hand, gameout of scopeDeclared out in step 1. Say so out loud; do not silently drop them.
joker, discard pileout of scopeSame.
The rule you will use forever

A noun becomes a class when it has state and behaviour of its own. It becomes an enum when the set of possible values is closed and known. It becomes an attribute when it only makes sense as part of something else. A verb becomes a method — and becomes a type only when it has interchangeable implementations.

05 Step 4 — One responsibility each

Every type in the design gets exactly one sentence — class, interface or enum, it makes no difference. Write it in the first person, as if the type were saying it. The test is mechanical: two uses of “and” means two types.

Nothing else gets a sentence. Attributes, out-of-scope nouns and noise all stay silent.

TypeKind“I am…” / “I…”
CardclassI am one playing card, identified by my suit and my rank.
DeckclassI hold an ordered pile of cards and hand them out from the top.
SuitenumI am one of the four suits, and I know my symbol and my colour.
Suit.ColourenumI am one of the two colours a suit can be.
RankenumI am one of the thirteen ranks, and I know my label and my comparison value.
ShufflerinterfaceI reorder a list of cards.
RandomShufflerclassI reorder a list of cards uniformly at random.
Where this list comes from — and why it is not just step 3

You start from the types step 3 produced, but expect the list to move in two directions.

Types get added. RandomShuffler was never a noun in the problem statement — go back and look. It exists because step 3 decided shuffling needed an interface, and an interface with no implementation does nothing at all. Every interface you create drags at least one concrete type in behind it.

Types get split. Step 4 is a test, and a test can fail. When a sentence needs two “and”s you split the type in two, and the design now contains a type that did not exist when step 3 finished.

What never earns a sentence: value (an attribute of Rank), player and joker (out of scope), top of the deck (noise). If you catch yourself writing a sentence for one of those, step 3 gave it the wrong verdict — go back and change it.

A thin sentence is information

Suit.Colour’s sentence is almost embarrassingly short, and that is the point: it tells you the type is a pure tag with no behaviour, which is exactly what a nested enum should be. Write the sentence even when it feels too obvious to bother with. The one time it comes out long and tangled for a type you assumed was simple is the time it has earned its keep.

An honest wobble

Deck’s sentence contains an “and”, and it also knows how to build a standard 52-card deck. That is arguably a third job. I am leaving it there today because splitting it now would cost more than it buys — but it is a real smell, and module 06 comes back to it with a factory. Noticing the wobble and choosing to live with it is design. Not noticing it is not.

06 Step 5 — Find the relationships

Four relationships, and each is a different strength of connection. This distinction is the most-asked UML question in interviews, so it gets its own module later.

BetweenKindWhy that one
Deck → Card Composition (filled diamond) The deck creates its own cards, nobody else holds that list, and the cards have no life outside it. Strongest form of “has-a”: owner dies, contents die.
Card → Suit, Rank Association (plain arrow) A card refers to enum constants it does not own. Enum constants are shared singletons — every ace of spades points at the same SPADES.
Deck → Shuffler Dependency (dashed arrow) The deck never stores a shuffler. It is handed one, uses it, forgets it. Weakest link there is — and that weakness is deliberate.
RandomShuffler → Shuffler Realisation (dashed, hollow triangle) It implements the interface.

07 Step 6 — Draw it

Read the diagram and notice what it tells you at a glance: Deck is the only class with a lot of methods, the enums are leaves, and the only dashed line in the design is the one place a decision can change later. A good class diagram makes the flexible part visible.

08 Step 7 — Code it

Four excerpts, each illustrating one decision from the steps above. Full sources are in 00-overview/src/deck/.

An enum that carries data and behaviour

Suit.java open file 
public enum Suit {

    CLUBS("♣", Colour.BLACK),
    DIAMONDS("♦", Colour.RED),
    HEARTS("♥", Colour.RED),
    SPADES("♠", Colour.BLACK);

    public enum Colour { RED, BLACK }

    private final String symbol;
    private final Colour colour;

    Suit(String symbol, Colour colour) {
        this.symbol = symbol;
        this.colour = colour;
    }

    public String symbol() { return symbol; }

    public Colour colour() { return colour; }
}

This is the payoff of calling suit an enum rather than a String. An invalid suit is now unrepresentable, and the symbol lives next to the thing it belongs to instead of in some SuitUtils.getSymbol(String) helper.

An immutable value object, written the long way

Card.java open file 
public final class Card {

    private final Suit suit;
    private final Rank rank;

    public Card(Suit suit, Rank rank) {
        this.suit = Objects.requireNonNull(suit, "suit");
        this.rank = Objects.requireNonNull(rank, "rank");
    }

    public Suit suit() { return suit; }

    public Rank rank() { return rank; }

    @Override
    public boolean equals(Object o) {
        if (this == o) return true;
        if (!(o instanceof Card)) return false;
        Card other = (Card) o;
        return suit == other.suit && rank == other.rank;
    }

    @Override
    public int hashCode() {
        return Objects.hash(suit, rank);
    }

    @Override
    public String toString() {
        return rank.label() + suit.symbol();
    }
}
Deliberately longhand

Java 17 collapses that entire file into public record Card(Suit suit, Rank rank) {} — constructor, accessors, equals, hashCode and toString, all generated. We are going to keep writing it the long way for a while, because record is only useful to you once you can say precisely what it generated. Shorthand after longhand, not instead of it.

The seam

Shuffler.java open file 
public interface Shuffler {

    void shuffle(List<Card> cards);
}

Three lines, and they are the only reason this design can absorb a new requirement without being edited. One abstract method also means any lambda of that shape is a Shuffler — which the demo exploits.

Ownership and a read-only view

Deck.java — excerpt open file 
public class Deck {

    private final List<Card> cards;

    private Deck(List<Card> cards) {
        this.cards = cards;
    }

    public static Deck standard52() {
        List<Card> cards = new ArrayList<>(52);
        for (Suit suit : Suit.values()) {
            for (Rank rank : Rank.values()) {
                cards.add(new Card(suit, rank));
            }
        }
        return new Deck(cards);
    }

    public void shuffle(Shuffler shuffler) {
        shuffler.shuffle(cards);
    }

    public Card deal() {
        if (isEmpty()) {
            throw new IllegalStateException("deck is empty");
        }
        return cards.remove(cards.size() - 1);
    }

    public List<Card> cards() {
        return Collections.unmodifiableList(cards);
    }
}

Three things to notice, each a design decision rather than a coding one. The constructor is private, so a deck can only be born through a named factory. The list is never handed in from outside, which is what makes the composition real rather than aspirational. And cards() returns an unmodifiable view, so callers can look without being able to reach in and reorder the pile.

Compiled with JDK 17 and executed. This is the actual output, not a sketch of it:

Two lines in that output are worth more than the rest. one == two ? false with one.equals(two) ? true is the whole point of a value object: two distinct objects, one card. And the reversal happened because of this single line in the demo — a complete change of shuffling policy, with Deck never recompiled:

DeckDemo.java — the seam in use
deck.shuffle(cards -> Collections.reverse(cards));

10 Step 9 — Critique it

Six weaknesses in my own design, with the module that fixes each. Doing this unprompted in an interview is the strongest signal you can send — it says you know the difference between “it works” and “it is good”.

WeaknessWhy it is a problemFixed in
Rank.value() hardcodes aces high That is a game rule living inside a deck type. Blackjack wants ace = 1 or 11. Module 05, Single Responsibility
Deck.standard52() means Deck builds itself Adding jokers or a pinochle deck means editing Deck, a class that should not care what is in it. Module 06, creational patterns
cards() returns a view, not a copy The caller cannot modify it — but it keeps changing under them as cards are dealt. Subtle, and a real bug source. Module 01, immutability
Shuffler is the Strategy pattern, unnamed Not a flaw — a sign. We arrived at a pattern by following requirements, which is the correct direction. Patterns are destinations, not starting points. Module 06, behavioural patterns
No discard pile, no reset A dealt card is gone forever. Fine for the stated scope; would not survive a real card game. Module 07, full problems
Not thread-safe Two threads calling deal() can receive the same card. Scoped out in step 1, on purpose and out loud. Module 07, full problems
