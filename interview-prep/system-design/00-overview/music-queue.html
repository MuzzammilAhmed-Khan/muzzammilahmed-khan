Module 00 · Overview

The same pipeline, in music

Taught · not drilled Session 1 · 2026-10-06 Worked example: the Up Next queue

The same nine steps as the deck of cards, run again on something you have actually used: the Up Next queue in a music app. Add songs, hit shuffle, play the next one.

Read this one if the cards meant nothing to you. Read both if you want to watch the point land twice: the steps do not change when the subject does.

Why two examples, not one

One worked example teaches you a deck of cards. Two teach you the method, because the only thing they have in common is the method. And these two do not agree on everything — one relationship comes out differently, for a reason worth more than either example on its own. That difference is step 5.

The nine steps, in short — the full version lives on the map:

1 clarify · 2 nouns · 3 filter · 4 responsibility · 5 relationships · 6 draw · 7 code · 8 run · 9 critique

01 Step 1 — Clarify the requirements

The prompt: “Design the Up Next queue in a music app.”

QuestionAnswer I will design to
Is this a saved playlist, or the Up Next queue?The queue. Playing a song takes it out of the line.
Can you add songs while it is playing?Yes, onto the end.
Do we actually play audio?No. We hand out the next song; something else plays it.
Repeat mode? Previous track?No. Out of scope.
Shuffle?Yes — and the algorithm must be swappable.
What does a song need to know about itself?Title, artist, genre, length. No album, no artwork.
Multiple devices or users sharing a queue?No. One queue, one thread.

In scope: hold an ordered line of songs, add to it, shuffle it, play the next one, report how many are left and how long that is, print a song readably, compare two songs.

Out of scope: audio playback, albums, artwork, repeat, previous, saved playlists, users, devices, persistence, concurrency.

Same lesson as last time

One answer changes the structure rather than the details: “shuffle must be swappable”. That single sentence is what forces an interface to exist. Everything else just fills in fields.

02 Step 2 — Extract the nouns

song · playlist · queue · title · artist · album · genre · duration · library · shuffle · play · next · repeat · user · device · volume

Sixteen nouns. Six types come out the other end — and two of those six are not on this list at all. Step 4 explains how they got in.

03 Step 3 — Filter the nouns

The row worth stopping on: artist

In this design, artist is a String on Song. In Spotify’s real design, Artist is certainly a class — it has followers, albums, a monthly listener count, a page you can open.

Both are correct. The verdict changed because the scope changed, not because the word changed. A noun is not a class because of what it is; it is a class because of what your system needs it to do. This is the single most common thing beginners get backwards.

Naming: why not Playlist?

Because playing a song removes it here, and a saved playlist does not shrink when you listen to it. If the name says playlist and the behaviour says queue, every future reader — including you in three weeks — will be confused by it. The name has to match the behaviour. Module 03 has a whole topic on this.

04 Step 4 — One responsibility each

Every type gets exactly one sentence, in the first person — class, interface or enum alike. Attributes, out-of-scope nouns and noise get nothing. Two uses of “and” means two types.

TypeKind“I am…” / “I…”
SongclassI am one song, identified by my title and my artist.
PlayQueueclassI hold an ordered line of songs and hand out the next one to play.
GenreenumI am one of a fixed set of genres, and I know my label and my energy.
Genre.EnergyenumI am how much energy a genre tends to have.
ShufflerinterfaceI reorder a list of songs.
RandomShufflerclassI reorder a list of songs uniformly at random.

Compare these to the deck’s sentences and notice they are the same sentences with the nouns swapped. That is the first sign you are learning a method rather than a domain.

Two of these were never nouns

Check the step 2 list: neither RandomShuffler nor Energy is on it. They arrived by different routes, and both routes are normal.

RandomShuffler exists because step 3 decided shuffling needed an interface, and an interface with no implementation does nothing. Every interface drags at least one concrete type in behind it.

Energy is less innocent. Nobody asked for it — step 1 said a song needs title, artist, genre and length, full stop. I invented it so Genre would carry data the way Suit does. Writing its sentence is what exposes it: once the type has to justify itself out loud, “I am how much energy a genre tends to have” immediately raises the question of who asked. See the critique.

Step 4 can also add a type: when a sentence needs two “and”s, you split, and the design gains a type that did not exist when step 3 ended. The step is a test, and tests are allowed to fail.

05 Step 5 — Find the relationships

Here is where the two examples part company, and it is the most valuable paragraph on this page.

Composition vs aggregation

A Deck creates its 52 cards. They exist nowhere else and they die with the deck. That is composition — a filled diamond.

A PlayQueue is handed songs that already exist in your library and go on existing after the queue is empty. That is aggregation — a hollow diamond.

Same picture, same arrow, one hollow shape instead of a filled one, and a completely different claim about who is responsible for whose lifetime.

BetweenKindWhy that one
PlayQueue → Song Aggregation (hollow diamond) Songs come from a library and survive the queue being drained. The queue borrows them.
Song → Genre Association (plain arrow) A song refers to an enum constant it does not own. Every pop song points at the same POP.
PlayQueue → Shuffler Dependency (dashed arrow) Handed one, uses it, forgets it. Never stored.
RandomShuffler → Shuffler Realisation (dashed, hollow triangle) It implements the interface.
The subtlety that trips people, and it is in the code

The queue does not own the songs — but it absolutely does own its own list of them. Your library order must not change because someone hit shuffle on the queue.

So PlayQueue.of(...) takes a copy of the list. Shared contents, private ordering. Aggregating the elements and owning the container are two different decisions, and most people only make one of them on purpose.

The value object — and a decision about identity

Song.java — excerpt open file 
public final class Song {

    private final String title;
    private final String artist;
    private final Genre genre;
    private final int seconds;

    public Song(String title, String artist, Genre genre, int seconds) {
        this.title = Objects.requireNonNull(title, "title");
        this.artist = Objects.requireNonNull(artist, "artist");
        this.genre = Objects.requireNonNull(genre, "genre");
        if (seconds <= 0) {
            throw new IllegalArgumentException("seconds must be positive: " + seconds);
        }
        this.seconds = seconds;
    }

    @Override
    public boolean equals(Object o) {
        if (this == o) return true;
        if (!(o instanceof Song)) return false;
        Song other = (Song) o;
        return title.equals(other.title) && artist.equals(other.artist);
    }

    @Override
    public int hashCode() {
        return Objects.hash(title, artist);
    }

    @Override
    public String toString() {
        return title + " - " + artist + " (" + duration() + ")";
    }
}
Notice what equals() does not use

Card compared every field it had. Song compares title and artist only — so a remaster at a different length counts as the same song.

That is a decision, not a default, and you have to be able to defend it. The critique section argues the opposite case. The real lesson: identity is designed, not discovered.

Borrowed contents, owned container

PlayQueue.java — excerpt open file 
public class PlayQueue {

    private final List<Song> songs;

    private PlayQueue(List<Song> songs) {
        this.songs = songs;
    }

    /** Defensive copy: the caller keeps their list, we get our own ordering. */
    public static PlayQueue of(List<Song> songs) {
        Objects.requireNonNull(songs, "songs");
        return new PlayQueue(new ArrayList<>(songs));
    }

    public void shuffle(Shuffler shuffler) {
        shuffler.shuffle(songs);
    }

    public Song playNext() {
        if (isEmpty()) {
            throw new IllegalStateException("queue is empty");
        }
        return songs.remove(0);
    }

    public List<Song> songs() {
        return Collections.unmodifiableList(songs);
    }
}

Two details carry the whole aggregation story. new ArrayList<>(songs) copies the list — so shuffling the queue cannot reorder your library. The copy contains the same Song objects — so the songs are shared, not duplicated. That is aggregation spelled out in one line.

And playNext() removes from the front, where the deck dealt from the end. A queue is first-in-first-out; a deck is a stack. On an ArrayList that makes this O(n) instead of O(1) — irrelevant at playlist sizes, and module 01 comes back to it when ArrayDeque appears.

Those last two lines are aggregation proved at runtime rather than asserted in a diagram. The queue was emptied completely; the library still holds all six songs. Had this been composition, draining the queue would have destroyed them.

Same code, opposite verdict

“Is Genre a good design?” feels like one question. It is three, and they do not all get the same answer:

DecisionVerdictWhy
Song has a genre fieldjustifiedStep 1 asked for it.
Genre is an enum, not a StringjustifiedClosed set; an invalid genre cannot be written down.
Genre carries label and Energynot justifiedNothing asked for it, and nothing reads it.

Now add a single requirement — “let me build a workout mix” — and Energy earns its place instantly. Same code, same nesting, same three constants, opposite verdict.

This is the same point as artist being a String here and an Artist class at Spotify: the verdict changes because the requirements changed, not because the code changed. You cannot judge a type by looking at it. You judge it against what the system was asked to do.

Two questions, not one

Every piece of state in a design faces two separate questions. They are very easy to collapse into a single one, and they can disagree:

  • Is it used? — grep for the getter. Mechanical; needs no judgement.
  • Was it asked for? — check it against step 1. Needs judgement.

Five accessors from this design, against the scope written down in step 1:

AccessorUsed?Asked for?Verdict
Song.duration()yes — Song.toString()yes — “print a song readably”keep
PlayQueue.totalSeconds()only the demoyes — “how long that is”keep
Song.genre()noyes — a song knows its genreflag, do not delete
Genre.label()nonodelete
Genre.energy()nonodelete

The demo does not count. PlayQueueDemo is a harness, not the product. Nothing in the system calls totalSeconds(), so question one is really “no” — it survives purely because step 1 asked for it. A demo call is evidence that something works, never evidence that it is needed.

Asked for but unread is a flag, not a crime. Song.genre() is stored on every song and read by nothing, yet step 1 did ask for it. So the question it raises is aimed at the requirement, not the code: a song knows its genre for what? Either a feature is missing or that requirement should never have been written. Raise it; do not delete it.

Grep is the screen; requirements are the verdict

Grep alone condemns three of those five — and two of the three are innocent. Grep hands you suspects; step 1 decides guilt.

Which is the concrete price of skipping step 1. Without requirements written down you cannot answer the second question at all, so you are left holding only the screen — and the screen convicts the innocent.

What Genre should actually be, today

Both of Genre’s fields fail both questions. So under the requirements as written, the honest version of the entire type is:

Genre.java — what the requirements actually pay for
public enum Genre { POP, ROCK, HIP_HOP, JAZZ, CLASSICAL, LOFI }

Notice exactly what survived: Genre itself. Step 1 did ask that a song know its genre, and a closed set is still the right shape for one. The type is justified; its contents are not. Three decisions, not one.

And each field has its own flip:

  • label() — “show the genre under the song title in the player.” HIP_HOP is not how you show a genre to a human, so the label earns its place at once.
  • Energy — “let me build a workout mix.”

Same enum, same six constants, opposite verdict — twice, for two different reasons.

What to take from this

Eight rows identical, two different. The eight are the method; the two are the domain. When you sit down to a problem you have never seen, you already have the eight — your real work is only ever finding the two.
